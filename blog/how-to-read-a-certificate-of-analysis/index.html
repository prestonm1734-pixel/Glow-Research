How to Read a Peptide Certificate of Analysis

HPLC purity, mass spec identity, endotoxin limits — what each line on a peptide COA actually means, and the red flags that separate a real COA from a marketing document.

Glow Research • July 7, 2026 • 7 min read

A certificate of analysis is the only objective evidence you have about what's actually in a vial. It's also the document most often faked, recycled across batches, or quietly replaced with a marketing sheet that looks official. Here's how to read one properly.

What a COA is supposed to be

A real COA is a report from an analytical lab describing tests run on one specific batch of material. Not the compound in general — the batch. That distinction is the whole point, and it's the first thing to check: if the certificate doesn't carry a lot number that matches the vial in your hand, it isn't telling you anything about your material.

Reading the line items

Identity (mass spectrometry)

Confirms the compound is what the label claims. Mass spec measures the molecular weight of the material and compares it to the theoretical weight of the target sequence. A COA should state the expected mass and the observed mass. If it just says "confirmed" with no numbers, that's a summary, not data.

Purity (HPLC)

High-performance liquid chromatography separates the sample into its components and reports the target peptide as a percentage of total peak area. This is the number most buyers focus on, and it's the number most often quoted without context.

What matters alongside the percentage: the chromatogram itself. A purity figure with no trace behind it can't be evaluated. The trace shows you where the impurities sit — a single close-eluting impurity is a different situation from a scatter of small unrelated peaks.

Endotoxin

Bacterial endotoxin, measured in EU/mg. Relevant for any research application involving cell culture, where endotoxin contamination can confound results entirely and produce inflammatory responses unrelated to the compound being studied.

Water content and appearance

Residual moisture affects both stability and the accuracy of your mass calculations — if 5% of the vial's weight is water, your concentrations are off by 5%. Appearance should describe a white to off-white lyophilized powder or cake. Discoloration is a flag.

Red flags

The single most common trick: one certificate reused across every lot of a compound. If the lot number on the COA is generic, missing, or identical to what a different customer received months ago, the document proves nothing about your vial.
  • No lot number, or a lot number that doesn't match the vial label
  • No test date — or a date years before your batch was produced
  • No named testing laboratory. "Third-party tested" with no third party named is a claim, not a citation
  • Summary values with no chromatograms. A purity number without the trace can't be verified
  • Impossibly round numbers. Real HPLC output isn't exactly 99.0% across every batch
  • COA available only on request, after purchase. Documentation that appears only after money changes hands is a sales tool

In-house versus third-party

Suppliers testing their own material have an obvious conflict of interest. That doesn't make in-house data worthless — but independent verification from a lab with no stake in the result is a materially stronger claim, and any supplier doing it will say who the lab is.

What to do with it

Record the lot number and its COA alongside your experimental data. If a result looks anomalous months later, the ability to trace it back to a specific characterized batch is often the difference between a resolvable question and an unreproducible one.
