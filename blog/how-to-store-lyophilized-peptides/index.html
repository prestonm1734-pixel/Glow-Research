How to Store Lyophilized Peptides in the Lab

Temperature, light, and moisture all degrade lyophilized peptides. A practical storage guide for research labs, covering long-term, working, and reconstituted vials.

Glow Research • July 14, 2026 • 6 min read

Lyophilized peptides are stable — but only relative to their reconstituted form. Left in the wrong conditions, a vial that arrived at 99.8% purity can drift measurably in weeks. Three variables do almost all of the damage: temperature, moisture, and light.

Why lyophilized form is the stable one

Freeze-drying removes the water that peptide bonds need in order to hydrolyze. What's left is an amorphous solid with very little molecular mobility, which is why a sealed lyophilized vial can sit for years while the same peptide in solution degrades in days to weeks.

That stability is conditional. The powder is hygroscopic — it will pull moisture out of ambient air the moment the seal is compromised or a cold vial is opened before it warms. Once water is reintroduced, the clock starts.

Storage by timeframe

Long-term (months to years)

Store at −20°C or colder, sealed, desiccated, and protected from light. At this temperature most research peptides remain within specification for well beyond a year. −80°C extends that further and is worth it for material you're holding as a reference standard, but for most working inventory it's diminishing returns.

Short-term (days to weeks)

Refrigeration at 2–8°C is adequate for vials in active use. Keep them in the original sealed container with desiccant rather than loose on a shelf.

After reconstitution

This is where most degradation actually happens. Reconstituted peptides should be refrigerated and used within a short window — typically days, not weeks, depending on the sequence and diluent. If you need longer, aliquot into single-use volumes and freeze, because repeated freeze-thaw cycling is far more destructive than a single freeze.

Avoid repeated freeze-thaw. Each cycle concentrates solutes at the ice boundary and exposes the peptide to mechanical stress. Aliquot once, thaw once. A peptide that survives twelve months at −20°C can lose significant integrity across a handful of thaw cycles.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Opening a cold vial. When a vial at −20°C is uncapped in a room-temperature lab, ambient humidity condenses directly onto the powder. You've now introduced water to a compound whose entire stability profile depends on its absence — and you won't see it happen.

Let sealed vials equilibrate to room temperature before breaking the seal. Ten to twenty minutes on the bench is usually enough. Centrifuge briefly before opening if powder has shifted to the stopper during transit.

Light and oxidation

Sequences containing tryptophan, methionine, or cysteine are photosensitive and prone to oxidation. Amber vials, foil overwrap, or simply keeping boxes closed addresses this — it costs nothing and removes a variable. Nitrogen-flushed vials, which displace oxygen in the headspace at the time of sealing, further reduce oxidative degradation before the vial is ever opened.

A practical checklist

  • Long-term inventory at −20°C or below, sealed with desiccant
  • Active vials at 2–8°C, still in original packaging
  • Equilibrate to room temperature before opening — always
  • Centrifuge briefly to collect powder before reconstituting
  • Aliquot reconstituted material; never refreeze a thawed working solution
  • Keep everything out of direct light
  • Log the lot number and reconstitution date on the vial
