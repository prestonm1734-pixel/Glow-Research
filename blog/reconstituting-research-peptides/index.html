Reconstituting Research Peptides: A Practical Guide

Choosing a diluent, calculating concentration, and avoiding the handling mistakes that denature peptides during reconstitution in a research setting.

Glow Research • June 28, 2026 • 8 min read

Reconstitution is where a well-characterized peptide most often gets damaged. The compound arrives intact and within specification, then loses integrity in the sixty seconds it takes to add diluent incorrectly. The technique is simple, but the failure modes are easy to miss.

Before you add anything

Let the vial reach room temperature while still sealed. Opening a cold vial condenses atmospheric moisture directly onto the powder. See our guide on storing lyophilized peptides for why that matters more than it sounds.

Then centrifuge briefly. Lyophilized cake frequently shifts to the stopper during shipping, and powder left clinging to the septum won't dissolve. A few seconds is enough to bring everything to the bottom.

Choosing a diluent

Bacteriostatic water (sterile water with 0.9% benzyl alcohol) is the standard choice for research work where the solution will be accessed more than once. The benzyl alcohol suppresses microbial growth, which sterile water alone does not.

Sterile water is appropriate for single-use preparations. Some sequences with poor aqueous solubility require a different approach: a small volume of dilute acetic acid or another solvent to get the peptide into solution first, followed by dilution into the working buffer. Check the solubility guidance for your specific compound rather than assuming water will work.

Never direct the diluent stream straight onto the powder. Let it run down the inside wall of the vial instead. A stream hitting the cake directly causes localized denaturation and foaming, and foam means the peptide is unfolding at the air-liquid interface.

Adding the diluent

Add your calculated volume slowly, down the vial wall, then leave it alone. Most peptides dissolve on their own within a few minutes.

If material remains after sitting, swirl gently or roll the vial between your palms. Do not shake or vortex. Mechanical agitation denatures peptides and generates foam, and foam is not cosmetic: it indicates protein unfolding at the interface.

Calculating concentration

The arithmetic is straightforward: peptide mass divided by diluent volume.

  • 10 mg peptide + 2 mL diluent = 5 mg/mL
  • 10 mg peptide + 1 mL diluent = 10 mg/mL
  • 5 mg peptide + 2 mL diluent = 2.5 mg/mL

Two things quietly break this calculation. First, net peptide content: the vial contains the stated mass of peptide plus counterions and residual moisture, so the actual peptide mass may be lower than the label figure. Your COA should state net peptide content if it was measured. Second, volume displacement: the dissolved solid adds to total volume, which is negligible at low concentrations and less so at high ones.

After reconstitution

  • Label the vial with concentration, diluent, and date, not just concentration
  • Refrigerate at 2–8°C and protect from light
  • Aliquot into single-use volumes if the solution will be stored rather than used promptly
  • Inspect before each use: cloudiness, precipitate, or discoloration means stop
  • Never refreeze a thawed working solution

Common failure modes

Powder won't dissolve. Give it more time before intervening. If it persists, the sequence likely needs a different diluent, not more agitation.

Solution went cloudy. Aggregation or precipitation. The material should not be used; cloudiness means a fraction of the peptide is no longer in the state you're trying to study.

Persistent foam. The solution was agitated too aggressively. Let it settle, and use gentler technique next time. Some of that peptide has already denatured.

Start from a known quantity

Every calculation above assumes the vial contains what the label says. That assumption is only as good as the documentation behind it, which is why net peptide content and batch-specific purity data matter before you reconstitute anything.
